# Give the Reader a voice and a memory

The two additions most worth making are **sentence audio (TTS)** and **a quiet progress layer built on words read and known words**. Every serious competitor has some form of each, and both fit this app's calm, self-hosted design without importing the guilt mechanics users complain about. Behind them comes a second tier of cheap features that make reading smoother. One is a **sentence translation/Explain action**: a Lute-style sentence Dictionary URL in the reading screen, with the Tutor's Explain for depth. The others are **an FSRS workload forecast with a backlog cap**, **an end-of-session summary card**, and **a "comfortable vs. study" split of the Fit range**. Anki/CSV export, share-target import and subtitle import are useful plumbing but matter less for engagement. AI level-adapted rewrites and generated stories should come last, as scaffolds with a verified coverage loop, because the one controlled study found AI simplification did not improve comprehension. The earlier quick list holds up with three revisions. Replace the "daily goal" with an opt-in weekly rhythm and a range goal, never a streak. Lead stats with *words read*, the most research-grounded metric, ahead of *known words*. Treat "AI rewrite" as a beginner bridge rather than a headline feature. This app already leads its closest peers on AI: Lute and LinguaCafe have no LLM features, and Lute deliberately has no SRS. The remaining gap is mostly audio and visible progress.

## Competitors converge on five gaps, and audio is the largest

Set against LingQ, Lute, LWT, Readlang and LinguaCafe, the Reader is missing five things the others commonly ship: audio, sentence translation, progress stats, Anki export and some form of habit tracking. Lute v3, the closest analogue, plays attached book audio with speed control and timeline bookmarks, but its manual admits there is **no TTS, no single-word audio and no audio-to-page sync** ([Lute manual](https://luteorg.github.io/lute-manual/print.html)). Its most-reacted open issues confirm the demand: audio synced to SRT subtitles (#648), TTS word pronunciation (#36), YouTube import (#73) and word-stats breakdowns (#178, #180) ([Lute issues](https://github.com/LuteOrg/lute-v3/issues?q=is%3Aissue+sort%3Areactions-%2B1-desc)). The HugoFara LWT fork already ships **TTS, audio sync, progress statistics, bulk translation and Anki export** ([HugoFara/lwt](https://github.com/HugoFara/lwt)). Readlang's 2024–2026 changelog is almost entirely voices and AI: server-generated Read-Aloud voices, MP3 upload with automatic transcription, YouTube sync, furigana/pinyin, Story Bot and "Reading Guides" ([Readlang forum updates](https://forum.readlang.com/c/updates/6)). Taken together, the industry is moving toward AI explanation plus audio. The Reader has the first half and none of the second.

User sentiment says what to copy and what to leave alone. A long Readlang forum post praises Readlang for keeping **texts whole rather than chopped into lessons**, for an Explain feature far better than LingQ's dictionaries, and for a frequency-ordered word list. The same user calls LingQ's library "distracting at best and anxiety-inducing" and its free plan "a joke", yet still found LingQ's **known-words count motivating** ([Readlang forum](https://forum.readlang.com/t/anyone-here-use-used-lingq-or-any-other-alternatives/123?page=4)). Trustpilot reviewers describe LingQ shipping half-finished updates instead of fixing bugs ([Trustpilot](https://ie.trustpilot.com/review/lingq.com)). The Reader's whole Books, editable Terms, Zen mode and ranked Suggestions already match what users praise. A visible count of progress is the clearest motivator it lacks. Lute has shipped only bugfix releases since March 2025 ([Lute releases](https://github.com/LuteOrg/lute-v3/releases)), which leaves room for a self-hosted reader that keeps improving.

## Priority 1: sentence audio, with a voice tiered per language

Audio is the biggest gap and the most technically tractable, but the four languages behave very differently. The right design is a **tiered TTS provider with per-language defaults and a server-side cache**.

| Language | Free in-browser (Web Speech) | Self-hosted offline | Best freely reachable quality |
|---|---|---|---|
| Russian | Good (Google русский, iOS Milena) | Piper: denis, dmitri, irina, ruslan | Microsoft ru-RU Dmitry/Svetlana via edge-tts |
| Chinese | Good (Google 普通话, iOS Tian-Tian etc.) | Piper huayan; Kokoro zh is graded D | Microsoft zh-CN Xiaoxiao, Yunxi and others |
| Farsi | Unverified per platform | Piper: amir, ganji, gyro, reza_ibrahim and others | Microsoft fa-IR Dilara/Farid |
| Bosnian | Unverified per platform | None for bs; Piper sr_RS serbski_institut is the nearest | Microsoft bs-BA Goran/Vesna |

Sources: [talkrapp on speechSynthesis](https://talkrapp.com/speechSynthesis.html), [Piper VOICES.md](https://github.com/rhasspy/piper/blob/master/VOICES.md), [Kokoro VOICES.md](https://huggingface.co/hexgrad/Kokoro-82M/blob/main/VOICES.md), [edge-tts voice list](https://github.com/FoloToy/folotoy-server-self-hosting/wiki/Edge%E2%80%90TTS%E2%80%90Voices), [Azure bs-BA Vesna](https://json2video.com/ai-voices/azure/voices/bs-ba-vesnaneural/).

Start with the **Web Speech API** in the reading screen: a play button per Sentence, word audio in the Term panel, and a 0.5–1.5× rate. It costs nothing and needs no server work. Expect its known quirks, though. `getVoices()` often returns empty until `voiceschanged` fires, Android reports tags like `ru_RU`, and iOS lists voices it cannot actually use ([talkrapp](https://talkrapp.com/speechSynthesis.html)). The Readium team's web-speech-recommended-voices project is the reference for choosing a default voice ([GitHub](https://github.com/HadrienGardeur/web-speech-recommended-voices/issues)). Because Farsi and Bosnian system voices are not guaranteed, add a **server-side provider** next. Generate audio once per Sentence, key it by a hash of the Normalised sentence text plus the voice, and store it on disk beside the SQLite file. Piper runs on CPU and covers ru, zh and fa offline. Note that the rhasspy repo was archived in October 2025 and some voice licenses are restrictive ([Piper](https://github.com/rhasspy/piper/blob/master/VOICES.md)). For Bosnian there is **no open self-hostable voice**. The realistic options are Microsoft's bs-BA neural voices through Azure or the unofficial edge-tts endpoint, or Piper's Serbian voice reading Latin-script Bosnian, which no source has tested ([immersion notes: Piper, json2video](https://json2video.com/ai-voices/azure/voices/bs-ba-goranneural/)). Farsi has a specific trap: it is written without short vowels, so homographs get misread. The Pronunciation field already holds a "vowelled spelling for Farsi". Feeding that, or a Tutor-vowelled sentence, to TTS is an untested but cheap mitigation.

Once audio is cached per Sentence it pays off three times. It can play during Practice, where FSRS cards already show sentences from your own Books. It can ride along in an Anki export. And it enables read-along highlighting via the Web Speech `boundary` event, whose reliability for zh and fa is unresearched. Lingopie's sentence loop and auto-pause are cheap additions for shadowing ([actualfluency](https://actualfluency.com/lingopie-review)). Book audio from a user's own audiobook is a later, larger step. WhisperX has alignment models for ru, zh, fa and hr, but none for bs or sr ([whisperX alignment.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/m-bain/whisperX/main/whisperx/alignment.py)).

## Priority 2: a quiet progress layer, built on words read

The research supports measuring progress but not loss-aversion mechanics. **Words read** is the most meaningful metric. Nation estimates that about **200,000 tokens** give 12+ encounters with words at the 2nd thousand level and about **3 million** at the 9th thousand ([Nation 2014](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1044345.pdf)). Cobb argues that about 500k words a year is a realistic L2 pace ([Cobb 2016](https://www.lextutor.ca/cv/cobb_rifl_2016.pdf)). That makes a milestone ladder (100k → 250k → 500k → 1M words read per Language) a ladder of real meaning, not a vanity count. Jeon & Day's meta-analysis of 49 extensive-reading studies found small-to-medium gains, larger for adults and for **web-based reading** ([Jeon & Day 2016](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/b52a5591-ec04-4026-816f-434361a4e899)). Known words is LingQ's flagship number, computed from status-4 plus Known ([LingQ support](https://lingq-support.groovehq.com/help/how-do-i-increase-my-known-words)), and users explicitly name it as motivating.

The schema already supports most of this. `page.read_at` plus a Page's Word count backfills words read per day. `review.reviewed_at` and the `schedule` table give an FSRS forecast and retention. `term.created_at` dates when each Term first appeared. What is missing is **status history**: a known-words-over-time line needs a small `status_change(term_id, from, to, at)` log, written in the shared domain code so both the web app and the MCP server record it (per ADR 0003). Known words can only be backfilled as "today's count". The stats page should show a single cumulative known-words line, words read as a weekly strip with no red "missed" styling, and a Lute-style per-Book status bar ([Lute overview](https://skivling.codeberg.page/lute)), which also belongs on Suggestion cards. It should also show a 7–14 day **FSRS Due forecast** and monthly true retention against target, framed as calibration. Anki's own docs warn that single-day retention is noise ([Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/manual/stats)). Readlang users have asked for exactly these per-day and unique-word counts ([Readlang forum](https://forum.readlang.com/t/previous-days-word-count/1890)).

On habits, the earlier "gentle daily goal" idea needs revising. Silverman & Barasch's seven experiments (>4,000 participants) found intact streaks motivate, but a **highlighted broken streak cuts persistence even when the break wasn't the user's fault**, and making the break more visible makes the harm worse ([INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive); [Psychology Today](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation)). Their recommendations are a broad definition, weekly windows, never surfacing breaks, and offering repair. Duolingo's own streak optimisations moved retention by only +1.7% and +0.38%, gains that matter at its scale and barely at one user's ([Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)). Range goals ("read 1,000–2,000 words") drew more re-engagement than single-number goals ([Scott & Nowlis, WashU](https://source.washu.edu/2013/06/why-is-it-easier-to-lose-24-pounds-rather-than-3-pounds/)), and encouragement works best near the **lower** end ([Wallace & Etkin 2024](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v184y2024ics0749597824000323.html)). The fitting design is therefore **opt-in, weekly and broad**. Show seven ink dots that fill on any day with reading, Reviews or a Tutor session, with an optional "aim for 4–6 days" and an optional words-read range. Missed days stay blank, never red, and there are no notifications. This follows Amber Case's calm-technology principles of using the periphery and informing without demanding ([Case](https://caseorganic.com/post/principles-of-calm-technology/)).

Two small features carry most of the emotional payoff. An **end-of-session card**, shown when you mark a Page Read or leave Zen mode, lists words read, new Terms, Terms promoted to Known and minutes, set like a colophon and dismissible. A **return-after-absence path** caps the Due queue, suggests a low-Unknown-share Book and says nothing about the gap. Review backlogs of 600–2,000 items are a documented quit trigger in SRS communities ([WaniKani forum](https://community.wanikani.com/t/my-cautionary-tale-about-vacation-mode/31755)), so a forecast-aware cap on new Learning Terms is an engagement feature, not just a scheduling detail.

## Priority 3: cheap reading-flow upgrades that reuse what exists

**Sentence translation** is the most common request this app lacks, and it does not need a new AI integration. Lute separates term dictionaries from **sentence dictionaries**, which are URLs that take the whole sentence and open embedded or in a popup, bound to `t` and `Shift+t` ([Lute manual](https://luteorg.github.io/lute-manual/print.html)). The Reader already has per-Language Dictionaries, so adding a "sentence" kind with a `###`-style placeholder fits the existing model. Users configure DeepL, Google Translate or Yandex per Language. Iframe-blocking sites are a recurring Lute pain (#652), so default sentence dictionaries to popup ([Lute issues](https://github.com/LuteOrg/lute-v3/issues?q=is%3Aissue+sort%3Areactions-%2B1-desc)). Deeper **Explain** belongs to the Tutor, since ADR 0003 deliberately keeps the LLM outside the web app. Explain is the most widely adopted and least controversial AI feature (LingQ "Explain This", Readlang AI Explain), and its failure modes are dictionary-like answers and wrong-context mix-ups ([Readlang forum](https://forum.readlang.com/t/how-do-i-use-the-explain-feature/5072)). The Tutor sees the Bookmark, the Sentence, Term statuses and Transfer, so it avoids both. The fitting change is a reading-screen action that moves the Bookmark to the Sentence and flags it for explanation. The existing "I'm here, help me" flow then picks it up, with no in-app API key.

**Split the Fit range into two bands.** The 5–15% Unknown-share window means 85–95% coverage. Hu & Nation put 95% as minimal and 98% as comfortable, while Schmitt et al. found a roughly **linear** relation with no hard threshold ([Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011](https://www.lextutor.ca/cover/papers/schmitt_etal_2011.pdf)). Day & Bamford's first extensive-reading principle is that material should be easy ([Day & Bamford 2002](https://www2.hawaii.edu/~readfl/rfl/October2002/day/day.html)). Offering a "comfortable" band (≤5% Unknown) beside a "study" band (5–15%) honours that principle and leaves the choice with the learner. Note the share is computed on distinct Words, not running tokens, so it reads harder than token coverage. Labelling it honestly avoids a false precision.

The other quick wins are small. A Kindle-style **"~8 min left"** estimate comes from your own words-per-minute per Language, measured between Bookmark moves ([TekRevue](https://www.tekrevue.com/tip/reset-remaining-chapter-book-time-kindle/)), and pairs naturally with Suggestion cards ("12 min · 6% new"). A guilt-free **"set aside"** state for Books follows Day & Bamford's freedom-to-abandon principle. **Interlinear glosses** work like Kindle Word Wise, which puts hints above hard words with a five-step density slider ([ecnomikata](https://ecnomikata.com/pr/4105/)). Here they would show the first Translation above Unknown and Learning 1–2 Words only, plus `<ruby>` pinyin from Pronunciation for Chinese, and are better targeted than Word Wise because the app knows your Statuses. A **"seen N times"** count in the Term panel reports how often a Word occurs in your Books, which Migaku's popup shows ([Migaku on X](https://x.com/MigakuOfficial/status/1729339652879110301)). It is a cheap query over stored Sentences, and its value is unmeasured. **Pre-teaching** a Book's key words has direct support: the NDL story creator pre-teaches up to three out-of-level keywords and recycles them ([NGSL AI tools](https://www.newgeneralservicelist.com/aitools)). The Tutor could do this from Frequency band × Unknown occurrences in the next Pages.

## Priority 4: plumbing for export and import, and AI generation kept honest

**Anki export** was Lute's top-reacted request before it shipped in 3.10.0 ([Lute issues](https://github.com/LuteOrg/lute-v3/issues?q=is%3Aissue+sort%3Areactions-%2B1-desc)). Here it matters less, because FSRS practice with in-book sentences already lives in the app. Build it in two steps. First, a CSV/TSV export of Term, Parent, Pronunciation, Translations, Sentence, a cloze Sentence (Yomitan's prefix/body/suffix split), the Book title and tags. It works for phone users, though media only travels with AnkiConnect ([Yomitan wiki](https://yomitan.wiki/anki/)). Second, a browser-side **AnkiConnect** client calling `localhost:8765` with `addNote`. Pass the cached sentence-audio URL so AnkiConnect downloads it, put a unique field first for duplicate checks, and tell users to add the app's origin to `webCorsOriginList`, as asbplayer requires ([asbplayer docs](https://docs.asbplayer.dev/docs/getting-started/mining-subtitles)). **Import** improvements in rough value order are as follows. A **PWA share target** lets "share → Reader" import a URL or text, with POST and a service worker for files; MDN marks it limited availability ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Progressive_web_apps/Manifest/Reference/share_target)), and iOS needs a bookmarklet or Shortcut fallback. **.srt/.vtt subtitle import** keeps timestamps for later audio sync. **Kindle `vocab.db`** is already SQLite with a context sentence per word ([DEV: Kindle to Anki](https://dev.to/psamim/how-i-transfer-my-vocabs-from-my-kindle-paperwhite-to-anki-1f4d)). Avoid server-side YouTube transcript scraping, because YouTube blocks cloud IP ranges and even bursts from home IPs ([youtube-transcript-api](https://pypi.org/project/youtube-transcript-api/)). Uploaded subtitle files sidestep that.

**AI-adapted text** ranks lower than its popularity suggests. LingQ has offered one-click simplification since October 2023, framed as "read the simple version, then the original" ([LingQ news](https://www.lingq.com/en/news/lingq-launches-ai-lessons/)). Murphy Odo's 2025 study, available as abstract only, found that once topic familiarity was controlled, **neither human nor AI simplification was easier to comprehend than the original**, and that AI simplification drops helpful cultural information ([LLT 29(1)](https://www.lltjournal.org/item-detail/1186/)). Generated stories draw the same complaints everywhere: one style, one moral, the recurring character "Carla", and English leaking into Arabic text ([Readlang Story Bot thread](https://forum.readlang.com/t/new-story-bot-for-ai-generated-stories/1309)). Story Bot verifies nothing about coverage, so pasted word lists end up "mostly" used. The Reader can do what almost no product does: **generate, segment with Intl.Segmenter, match Words to Terms via Normalised form and Parent, compute the Unknown share, then regenerate or substitute until it lands in the comfortable band**, with a few chosen Learning Terms deliberately included. This is the SRS-Stories design, which beat constrained decoding in English, Chinese and Polish ([arXiv 2512.18362](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.18362)), and the NGSL Profiler's "analyse → adjust → re-check" loop ([NGSL](https://www.newgeneralservicelist.com/aitools)). Because the generated text is Russian, Farsi or Bosnian, the check has to run on Parents, not surface forms. It belongs in the Tutor as an MCP tool (`check_coverage(text, language)`) that imports the result as a Book. Use it for beginner bridges, a pre-read retelling of a hard Page, or a review story for Due Terms, never as a replacement for authentic Books. No source checks LLM quality for Bosnian or Farsi, so validate on those first.

## What to leave out

The evidence argues against several familiar features. Loss-framed streaks, push reminders and guilt copy are out: broken-streak salience harms persistence, and Duolingo's notification and emotional-visual patterns were classed as deceptive design ([Castro & Valença 2025](https://deceptive.design/articles/teaching-or-manipulating-on-the-adoption-of-bright-and-deceptive-patterns-by-duolingo)). Coins, badges and leaderboards are out too, because the motivational and behavioural effects of gamification are the least stable, while cognitive effects hold up ([Sailer & Homner 2020](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056)). So make learning visible rather than adding points. Splitting Books into "lessons" and adding a news-style library feed are the two LingQ choices users most resent. Term images via web search have weak evidence and real maintenance costs: Lute's Bing image feature needed async rewrites and a random-image bug fix ([Lute releases](https://github.com/LuteOrg/lute-v3/releases)). Toucan-style word swapping into English pages is criticised for single-word, context-blind replacement ([gHacks](https://www.ghacks.net/2022/07/02/toucan-can-you-learn-a-language-while-browsing-the-web/)) and sits outside the app's purpose. Conversation chatbots have only small-sample, mostly anxiety-related evidence ([ReCALL meta-analysis](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/31847710516602398819C5E594038E7B/S0958344025100268a.pdf/dialogue-based-computer-assisted-language-learning-systems-for-second-language-speaking-development-a-three-level-meta-analysis.pdf)). Discussing the text just read with the Tutor gets most of the benefit at no new cost. A full browser extension, like Language Reactor's, breaks every time a site changes its UI ([LLN forum](https://forum.languagelearningwithnetflix.com/t/kamal-hinduja-swiss-what-is-language-reactor-and-how-does-it-work/33877)). A share target and bookmarklet cover import at a fraction of the upkeep.

## Conclusion

The strongest finding is that this app's gaps are mostly about **feedback**, not intelligence. It already does the hard, differentiating things: lemma-aware Statuses, FSRS with in-book sentences, and an AI tutor that knows what you know. What it doesn't do is let you hear the text or see your progress. Words read and known words turn invisible learning into a slow, visible accumulation that matches Nation's volume targets. That is the motivation users credit LingQ with, without the streak anxiety they blame Duolingo for. Audio is harder for Bosnian and Farsi than for Russian and Chinese. A cached, tiered TTS layer turns that weakness into one Microsoft-voice call per Sentence, and the same cache then feeds Practice, Anki and read-along.

The less obvious takeaway is that the Reader's data model gives it a defensible edge in AI generation that the commercial apps lack. They prompt for a level and hope. This app can **measure** a generated text's Unknown share against your actual Terms and Parents and reject it until it fits. That turns generated text from the weakest-evidence feature into a controlled tool, if it is kept as a scaffold back to real Books. The open questions to settle with quick experiments are whether Piper's Serbian voice reads Bosnian acceptably, whether Web Speech `boundary` events fire for Chinese and Farsi, and whether Claude's output quality and Intl.Segmenter tokenisation hold up for Farsi and Bosnian coverage checks.
