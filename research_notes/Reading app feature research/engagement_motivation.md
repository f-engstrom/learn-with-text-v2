# Engagement and motivation in self-directed reading-based language learning

Scope: which engagement mechanics (streaks, goals, progress visualisation, gentle habit design, reading-UX niceties) suit a calm, self-hosted "learn by reading" app with FSRS, word statuses, Suggestions ranked by 5–15% unknown-word share, and an AI tutor. Research window preferred 2015–2026; older classics are marked **[classic]**.

Source-quality note: Duolingo's own numbers are company-reported A/B results (not peer-reviewed). Several web results were SEO or growth-blog summaries (e.g. Lazyweb, Substack growth breakdowns, a student PDF claiming "40% higher Day-30 retention"); they are excluded or flagged below.

---

## 1. Streaks, daily goals, gamification and intrinsic motivation

### Takeaway
Streaks do motivate people while they last. The best evidence (Silverman & Barasch, JCR 2022/23, 7 experiments, >4,000 participants) shows that a highlighted *broken* streak makes people less likely to continue, even when the break was outside their control. Repair options, broader definitions and weekly windows soften the effect. Gamification helps learning on average, but the motivational effects are the least stable. Qualitative SDT studies of Duolingo keep finding streak-loss frustration and pressure alongside the benefits. For a calm app the evidence points to *forgiving, process-based, optional* consistency signals, not loss-aversion mechanics.

### Cited Findings
**Streak psychology (peer-reviewed)**
- Silverman & Barasch, "On or Off Track: How (Broken) Streaks Affect Consumer Decisions", *Journal of Consumer Research* (2022). Across 7 experiments (>4,000 participants), highlighting an intact streak in an app log made people more likely to continue, even with identical actual behaviour. Example: 66.23% with an intact streak chose another strength exercise vs 57.86% with a broken one. Highlighting also raised feelings of accomplishment, continued app use and willingness to recommend. — [INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive)
- In the same study, a broken streak reduced persistence even when the break had external causes. The effect held regardless of streak length, and *salience magnified the harm of broken streaks*. The negative effect faded with temporal distance from the break. — [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/ulterior-motives/202306/how-broken-streaks-sap-motivation); [Silverman faculty page](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/profile/jasilv)
- The harm was amplified when people felt personally responsible for the break, and it shrank when people could repair the streak. Almost half would watch an ad to maintain or repair a streak. — [INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive)
- The authors' design recommendations:
  - Don't highlight or notify users about broken streaks.
  - Define streaks broadly, so several activities count.
  - Use flexible windows, such as weekly instead of daily, but not so loose that they stop motivating.
  - Frame unavoidable breaks as externally caused.
  - Offer repair.

  — [INSEAD Knowledge](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive)

**Duolingo's own published claims (company blog; self-reported A/B tests)**
- Over 6 million people had a 7+ day streak (Jan 2022). — [Duolingo blog, Mansur 2022](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- A streak-extension animation, plus special milestone animations, raised 7-day retention of brand-new learners by +1.7%. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Letting learners equip two Streak Freezes at once raised daily active learners by +0.38% (relative), and Duolingo says it did not encourage more days off. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Duolingo justifies freezes with a UPenn/UCLA study finding that some "slack" in goal pursuit motivates more than rigid rules. The blog does not name the authors; the link suggests 2018. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Learners who reach a 7-day streak are 3.6× more likely to complete their course. This is correlational; self-selection is likely. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit); [Duolingo streak research page](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-streak-research/)
- The blog names loss aversion as a motivating bias, and its screenshots show freezes refilled for in-app currency (400 gems). Monetised loss aversion is exactly the pattern critics object to. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Secondary report: Duolingo tested a lower daily bar and saw more than 40% more people keep a 7-day streak. This shows the metric is easy to game by design and may not track learning. — summarised via [search of Duolingo/Mobbin sources](https://mobbin.com/blog/streaks-are-about-losing-a-study-of-859-streak-designs) (unverified primary)

**Habit formation [classic-adjacent, 2010]**
- Lally et al.'s habit-formation research found that missing a single opportunity did not materially affect habit formation. That argues a rigid "don't break the chain" rule is unnecessary. — cited via third-party summary [Mobbin](https://mobbin.com/blog/streaks-are-about-losing-a-study-of-859-streak-designs) (primary paper not fetched)

**Goals: range goals beat single-number goals**
- Scott & Nowlis, *JCR* 2013, found that people were more likely to re-engage with a goal after a high-low *range* goal (e.g. "lose 2–4 lb") than after a single number. The mechanism is that the low end makes the goal attainable and the high end makes it challenging, so reaching it feels like more of an accomplishment. The effect replicated for puzzles, resisting tempting food and a shopping game. Actual performance did not differ, but re-enrolment rose. — [WashU Source](https://source.washu.edu/2013/06/why-is-it-easier-to-lose-24-pounds-rather-than-3-pounds/); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-670766.html)
- Wallace & Etkin (2024, *OBHDP*), "Range goals as dual reference points", found that performance on range goals is greatest when encouraging cues come around the *lower* endpoint. — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v184y2024ics0749597824000323.html)

**Gamification meta-analysis**
- Sailer & Homner (2020), *Educational Psychology Review* 32:77–112, found that gamification has positive effects on cognitive, motivational and behavioural outcomes. Only the *cognitive* effect was stable in high-rigour studies; motivational and behavioural effects were less stable. Game fiction and social interaction moderated behavioural outcomes. — [Uni Augsburg OPUS](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/frontdoor/index/index/docId/109056); [ERIC record](https://www.fachportal-paedagogik.de/literatur/vollanzeige.html?FId=eric_ej1245270)

**SDT studies of language apps (mostly small or qualitative)**
- A 2026 qualitative thesis (7 university students using Duolingo for English) found that app features supported autonomy, competence and relatedness. Students also reported frustration from losing streaks, competitive pressure and repetitive tasks. — [UMPO eprints](https://eprints.umpo.ac.id/21371/)
- A 2024 Arabic-vocabulary Duolingo study reported a significant rise in motivation, which the authors linked to independence, competence and connection. It is small-scale, with no long-term follow-up. — [Al-Wijdan journal](https://ejournal.uniramalang.ac.id/index.php/alwijdan/article/view/5844)
- Jeon (2022) used SDT to examine how elements of a self-directed conversational-agent app supported or thwarted basic psychological needs in young Korean EFL learners. — [selfdeterminationtheory.org PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/2022_Jeon_ExploringaSelf-Directed.pdf)

**Dark-pattern critiques**
- Castro & Valença (2025, IHC, Brazilian Symposium on Human Factors in Computing Systems), "Teaching or Manipulating?", analysed Duolingo against fair and deceptive design criteria. They found clear, fair interfaces alongside deceptive elements: excessive notifications to maintain engagement and emotionally charged visuals. — [SOL/SBC](https://sol.sbc.org.br/index.php/ihc/article/view/37677); [deceptive.design summary](https://deceptive.design/articles/teaching-or-manipulating-on-the-adoption-of-bright-and-deceptive-patterns-by-duolingo)
- Opinion: a TU Eindhoven column calls Duolingo's owl a guilt-trip mechanic. — [Cursor TU/e](https://cursor.tue.nl/en/opinion/alain-starke/guilt-tripped-by-an-owl). This is commentary, not evidence.

### Inferences
- For this app, the most defensible "consistency" mechanic has these features:
  - (a) It is opt-in.
  - (b) It is weekly or "N of 7 days" rather than strictly daily.
  - (c) It counts any meaningful activity: reading, reviews or tutor.
  - (d) It never notifies or shows a broken state prominently. Show "you read on 4 of the last 7 days", not "streak lost".
  - (e) It offers repair or built-in slack, e.g. 1–2 rest days per week that need no currency.
- Range goals ("read 1,000–2,000 words today") are a cheap, evidence-backed upgrade over single-number daily goals, and they suit a calm tone. Put the visual encouragement at the low end, as in "goal reached" once the minimum is met.
- Duolingo's measured gains from its streak tweaks are small (+0.38%, +1.7%). Those matter at Duolingo's scale but say little about a personal app with one motivated user. The cost (anxiety) is borne by the individual.
- Because gamification's *motivational* effects are the least stable while its learning effects are more robust, mechanics should make learning visible rather than add extrinsic points and badges.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve Sailer & Homner's exact pooled effect sizes, because the Augsburg PDF failed to load. My recollection is roughly g≈0.49 cognitive, 0.36 motivational and 0.25 behavioural; verify before quoting.
- I found no peer-reviewed Duolingo paper on streaks. Duolingo's research site focuses on learning outcomes and Birdbrain, which I didn't fetch.
- I did not fetch the primary paper on the UPenn/UCLA "slack" study (likely Sharif & Shu, "emergency reserves"); it is cited only via Duolingo.
- The claim that a 2021 *Computers in Human Behavior* study links rigid streaks to guilt and abandonment appears only on fitness-app blogs and could not be verified.
- I fetched no study on overjustification or undermining effects specific to language apps. The classic Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 meta-analysis is relevant **[classic]** but was not sourced here.

## 2. Extensive reading research: what sustains reading

### Takeaway
The extensive reading (ER) literature centres on easy material, wide choice, freedom to abandon texts, and *volume*. Meta-analytic evidence shows small-to-medium gains, larger for adults, in EFL settings and for web-based reading. Nation's corpus work turns "volume" into concrete targets: hundreds of thousands to millions of running words. That makes a "words read" counter a theoretically meaningful metric, not a vanity number. The app's 5–15% unknown-word Suggestions sit on the harder side of ER's "easy" principle (95–98% coverage).

### Cited Findings
- Day & Bamford's "Top ten principles for teaching extensive reading" (2002) **[classic]**:
  1. The material is easy.
  2. A wide variety of material on many topics is available.
  3. Learners choose what to read, and they are not compelled to finish uninteresting material.
  4. Learners read as much as possible.

  The authors note that easy material is controversial because of a "pervasive view" that real-world texts should be used. — [Reading in a Foreign Language, Day & Bamford 2002](https://www2.hawaii.edu/~readfl/rfl/October2002/day/day.html); [Wikipedia: Extensive reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extensive_reading)
- Jeon & Day (2016) meta-analysis, *Reading in a Foreign Language* 28(2): 246–265, pooled 71 samples from 49 studies (1980–2014), with N=5,919. It found small-to-medium effects on reading proficiency. Adults showed larger effects than children or adolescents, EFL settings beat ESL, **web-based stories outperformed paper books**, and ER integrated into the curriculum had the largest effect. — [ScholarSpace, U. Hawaiʻi](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/b52a5591-ec04-4026-816f-434361a4e899); [NFLRC](https://nflrc.hawaii.edu/rfl/item/354)
- Nation (2014), "How much input do you need to learn the most frequent 9,000 words?", found that about 200,000 tokens give ≥12 repetitions at the 2nd 1,000-word level and about 3 million tokens do so at the 9th 1,000 level. That is roughly 2–25 novels at 120k tokens each. Nation considers this manageable at about 1,000 word families per year. — [ERIC EJ1044345](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1044345.pdf)
- Nation reports that about 8,000–9,000 word families are needed for 98% coverage of unsimplified text. Graded readers need about 2,000 families for 95% and 3,000 for 98% coverage (Webb & Macalister). — [Cambridge, Learning Vocabulary in Another Language](https://resolve.cambridge.org/core/books/learning-vocabulary-in-another-language/vocabulary-and-reading-and-writing/F45A4D6A40EAB3F1AD2048EC16671854); [lextutor, Cobb 2016](https://www.lextutor.ca/cv/cobb_rifl_2016.pdf)
- Cobb (2016) critique: 1 million words a year is normal for L1 schoolchildren reading self-selected texts, but about 500k a year is more realistic for L2 learners. Nation's volume figures have no published empirical validation for EFL. — [Cobb, RFL 2016](https://www.lextutor.ca/cv/cobb_rifl_2016.pdf)
- Practitioner example: a Lute user logged 9,485 words read over 11 days in a month. This illustrates how learners self-track words read. — [skivling blog](https://skivling.codeberg.page/te-reo-feb)

### Inferences
- **Words read** is the single most research-grounded progress metric for this app, because Nation's thresholds give it meaning. A gentle cumulative milestone ladder (100k → 250k → 500k → 1M words read) maps directly onto the literature. Per-day or per-week totals can use range goals.
- The 5–15% unknown-word window corresponds to only 85–95% coverage. ER theory would push a "comfortable/flow reading" tier at ≤5% unknown (≥95%), with 10–15% as "intensive/study" reading. That would honour Principle 1 without removing challenge. Exposing that choice preserves autonomy (SDT) and Principle 3.
- The "don't have to finish" principle suggests an explicit, guilt-free "set aside" state for texts, rather than leaving them as "in progress" forever.
- Web-based stories outperforming paper is weak support for interactive digital reading, such as glosses and lookups.

### Gaps
- I didn't find a direct volume moderator in Jeon & Day (2016) in the snippets.
- No primary source on Krashen's Free Voluntary Reading (*The Power of Reading*, 1993/2004 **[classic]**) was fetched. Its claims about pleasure reading and self-selection are widely cited but not sourced here.
- I found no empirical study on whether *displaying* a words-read counter increases reading volume in L2 learners. The link is inferred from the goal-setting and progress literature.

## 3. Progress visualisations that help (examples from LingQ, Lute, Anki/FSRS, WaniKani, StoryGraph)

### Takeaway
Reading-first language apps converge on a small set of metrics: known-word count, words read, new "LingQs"/words saved per day, and per-text status bars. SRS tools add retention and workload forecasts. Book trackers allow goals by books, pages *or hours*, with an annual wrap-up. The most useful for a calm app are cumulative, monotonic-feeling measures (known words, words read) and forward-looking workload forecasts, which prevent review-backlog anxiety. Retention stats are informative but noisy day-to-day.

### Cited Findings
**LingQ**
- Users pick a daily LingQ goal of 13, 25, 50 or 100 new words saved, changeable later in Home Settings. Hitting it daily maintains a streak. — [LingQ support: streak](https://lingq-support.groovehq.com/help/what-s-the-streak-and-how-do-i-maintain-it); [adjusting goal](https://lingq-support.groovehq.com/help/adjusting-your-daily-lingqs-goal)
- The Known Words count = status-4 "Learned" words + words marked Known. Learned words still appear in SRS; Known words do not. — [LingQ support: increase known words](https://lingq-support.groovehq.com/help/how-do-i-increase-my-known-words)
- A weekly status report sits on the right of the homepage, and a full statistics summary is in the profile. Users can also manually record listening and reading done outside the app. — [Understanding LingQ statistics](https://lingq-support.groovehq.com/help/understanding-lingq-statistics); [record listening and reading](https://lingq-support.groovehq.com/help/how-do-i-record-my-listening-and-reading)

**Lute (open-source, self-hosted, closest analogue to this app)**
- Lute v3 (Python/Flask) has a pop-up dictionary and statistics. The text list shows title, tags, word count, last read and a coloured **status bar** giving the proportion of words in each status. — [PyPI lute3](https://pypi.org/project/lute3/); [Lute overview](https://skivling.codeberg.page/lute)
- Lute's older stats-calculation wiki is deprecated in favour of the v3 manual. — [GitHub wiki](https://github.com/jzohrab/lute/wiki/Stats-calculation)

**Anki / FSRS**
- Anki's stats include a **True Retention** table across cards and time frames ("mature" = interval ≥21 days). With FSRS, true retention should sit close to desired retention. Single days are noisy, so monthly figures are more meaningful. The Future Due graph is FSRS-aware. — [Anki manual: stats](https://docs.ankiweb.net/manual/stats)
- The FSRS Helper add-on reschedules from full history, can spread cards that push a day over its review limit (at a small retention cost), and adds FSRS stats. — [fsrs4anki-helper GitHub](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/fsrs4anki-helper)
- A community proposal would store retrievability at review time so that *expected* and *actual* retention can be compared, since backlogs and changes to desired retention distort the figures. This is not yet a confirmed feature. — [Anki forums](https://forums.ankiweb.net/t/store-retrievability-in-review-logs/65374.md)

**WaniKani (SRS backlog pain)**
- Community threads repeatedly describe review backlogs of 600–2,000+ items after breaks. Vacation mode freezes reviews, and some users use it "surgically" between sessions. Common advice is to stop new lessons until the queue clears. Others say vacation mode desynchronises the SRS from actual knowledge. — [WaniKani: cautionary tale](https://community.wanikani.com/t/my-cautionary-tale-about-vacation-mode/31755); [huge backlog thread](https://community.wanikani.com/t/huge-backlog-for-the-new-year-a-lesson-in-using-vacation-mode/54965); [using vacation mode when overwhelmed](https://community.wanikani.com/t/using-vacation-mode-when-overwhelmed/55924)

**StoryGraph (reading-tracker reference)**
- Reading goals can be set in books, pages *or reading hours*. Stats can be filtered by time period, fiction/nonfiction, tags, mood and genre. An annual Reading Wrap-up summarises pages, books, average length and genres. — [Wikipedia: The StoryGraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_StoryGraph); [PopSci](https://www.popsci.com/diy/storygraph/)
- Users request page- or minute-based charts because the default charts count books. Granularity mismatches between goals and charts annoy users. — [StoryGraph roadmap request](https://roadmap.thestorygraph.com/requests-ideas/posts/offer-charts-based-on-number-of-pages-read-or-minutes-listened-i)

### Inferences
- A minimal, calm stats page for this app could contain:
  1. Known words over time, as a single quiet line chart. It grows monotonically and is LingQ's flagship metric.
  2. Words read: a cumulative total, plus a weekly bar or heat strip with no red or "missed" styling.
  3. A per-text status bar à la Lute. It already fits the Suggestions model, so show it on Suggestion cards.
  4. An FSRS review forecast for the next 7–14 days, so the user can see load coming and throttle new words. This is the antidote to WaniKani-style backlog dread.
  5. Monthly true retention vs target, framed as calibration rather than score.
  6. An optional end-of-year or end-of-month "wrap-up" in StoryGraph style.
- Coverage of a frequency list ("you know 78% of the top-2,000 words") would connect directly to Nation's coverage thresholds. Use it only if the app has a frequency list per language.
- Backlog management (cap new words when forecast load is high; "catch-up" mode) is an engagement feature, because review overload is a documented quit trigger in SRS communities.

### Gaps
- I couldn't verify whether Lute v3 has a words-read-per-day chart; the v3 manual was not fetched.
- I found no source for Bunpro's vacation mode or stats, Readwise's daily-review streak, or Migaku's progress features.
- I found no controlled studies on which progress visualisations improve persistence in L2 reading apps. The recommendations are product-practice and theory based.

## 4. Calm technology and gentle habit design

### Takeaway
Calm technology (Weiser & Brown 1995 **[classic]**; Amber Case 2015) argues for technology that uses the periphery, requires minimal attention, informs without demanding, and fails gracefully. Combined with the streak research, that means ambient, glanceable progress, no push or guilt notifications, slack built into goals, and positive "end of session" moments rather than loss warnings.

### Cited Findings
- Weiser & Brown coined "calm technology" in "Designing Calm Technology" (Xerox PARC, 1995) as a response to increasingly demanding IT. — [Wikipedia: Calm technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calm_technology)
- Amber Case's eight principles (*Calm Technology*, O'Reilly 2015) cover:
  1. Requiring the smallest possible amount of attention.
  2. Informing and creating calm.
  3. Using the periphery.
  4. Amplifying the best of technology and humanity.
  5. Communicating without speaking.
  6. Working even when failing.
  7. Using the minimum technology necessary.
  8. Respecting social norms.

  — [O'Reilly book page](https://oreilly.com/library/view/~/9781491925874); [Case: principles of calm technology](https://caseorganic.com/post/principles-of-calm-technology/); [Adobe blog 2016](https://blog.adobe.com/en/publish/2016/10/12/calm-technology-in-the-era-of-experience-design)
- Streak repair and broad or weekly windows reduce the demotivating effect of breaks. Breaks should not be made salient. — [INSEAD Knowledge (Silverman & Barasch)](https://knowledge.insead.edu/marketing/consumer-streaks-are-motivating-key-keeping-them-alive)
- Duolingo's streak animation and milestone moments measurably improved early retention (+1.7%). Celebration moments work, and they can be designed quietly. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit)
- Critique and design-alternative pieces propose reframing Duolingo streaks for "healthier and flexible" learning. — [UX Collective: Reframing streaks on Duolingo](https://uxdesign.cc/3-reframing-streaks-on-duolingo-5-ideas-for-a-more-healthy-and-flexible-approach-to-language-8fd89545771e); [Mobbin: study of 859 streak designs](https://mobbin.com/blog/streaks-are-about-losing-a-study-of-859-streak-designs)

### Inferences (concrete patterns that fit a paper or Zen aesthetic)
- **End-of-session summary card**, shown on closing a text or leaving Zen mode. It would list words read this session, new words met, words promoted (e.g. 3 → known), and minutes. Typeset it like a colophon or library-card stamp, without confetti. It should be dismissible and never block.
- **Weekly rhythm instead of a streak**: seven small dots or ink marks for the week, filled on days with any reading or reviews, and never marking a missed day in red. Optionally "aim for 4–6 days", a range goal.
- **Milestones as marginalia**, e.g. a one-line note "You've now read 100,000 words in Spanish" at natural pauses (end of text), not as interruptions mid-reading.
- **No push notifications by default.** If reminders are ever added, make them opt-in, user-scheduled and neutral, e.g. "Your next text is ready". Never use guilt copy.
- **Graceful failure after absence**: on return after a gap, cap the review queue, suggest an easy text (low unknown share), and say nothing about the gap. This matches "work even when failing" and the finding that reminding people of breaks hurts persistence.
- **Ambient progress**: a thin progress line or the status-colour bar on each text, a quiet "known words" figure in a corner of the library. The information sits in the periphery.

### Gaps
- I didn't fetch the UX Collective and Mobbin articles in full, so their specific design proposals are not detailed here.
- I found no empirical study of "end-of-session summary" effects in learning apps. The recommendation rests on goal-progress feedback and Duolingo's celebration result.

## 5. Small reading-UX niceties users love

### Takeaway
E-reader conventions users value include personalised "time left in chapter or book" (learned from reading speed), typography controls, clear position in the book, and local or offline operation. For an L2 reading app, the time estimate should be calibrated per language and difficulty.

### Cited Findings
- Kindle's "Time left in chapter" shows estimated minutes to the end of the chapter, based on personal reading speed. It is reported as usually accurate. — [TekRevue](https://www.tekrevue.com/tip/reset-remaining-chapter-book-time-kindle/); [BGR](https://www.bgr.com/2166895/tips-reading-more-kindle/)
- Per 2012–2014 reviews, Kindle computes this on device from words per page and page-turn timing, plus book type and previous sessions. A reset shows a "learning reading speed" message. Reading speed was reportedly stored only on the device. These are older claims. — [the-ebook-reader blog](https://blog.the-ebook-reader.com/2012/09/28/kindle-paperwhite-user-guide-and-new-video-surface/); [Literature & Latte forum](https://forum.literatureandlatte.com/t/epub-file-missing-x-min-left-in-chapter/154395); [Kaedrin](https://kaedrin.com/weblog/?p=372)
- A columnist found Kindle's estimate barely changed when he read more slowly, so personalisation may be coarse. — [Goodreads author blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/19325480-kindle-s-down-to-earth-estimates-of-reading-speed)
- Kindle offers reading-progress display options (time left in chapter or book, page, location) via the Aa menu. — [BGR](https://www.bgr.com/2166895/tips-reading-more-kindle/)
- Readlang users request per-day and unique-word counts, which shows demand for reading-volume stats in L2 reading tools. — [Readlang forum: previous day's word count](https://forum.readlang.com/t/previous-days-word-count/1890); [Readlang forum: unique words](https://forum.readlang.com/t/add-unique-words-count/1306)

### Inferences
- A "~6 min left in this text" estimate, computed locally from the user's own words-per-minute *in that language*, helps users choose a text that fits the time they have. It extends Suggestions naturally, e.g. "12 min · 8% new words". Keeping it local fits a self-hosted, privacy-respecting ethos.
- Reading speed (WPM) over time is a meaningful fluency indicator for L2 learners. Show it gently as a trend, not a score, because it varies with text difficulty. Normalise it, or show it alongside unknown-word share.
- Other low-cost wins that fit a paper look:
  - Font, size, line height and width controls.
  - Remembered position, with "continue reading" on the home screen.
  - An end-of-text recap, which the AI tutor could offer as an optional summary or comprehension question.
  - A guilt-free "set aside" state.
  - PWA/offline reading.

### Gaps
- I found no official current Amazon documentation for Kindle reading-time features; the sources are older blogs and forums.
- No sources were fetched on PWA/offline usage in reading apps, Apple Books/Kobo reading-goal features, or Readwise. These remain product-knowledge assumptions.
- I found no research on whether reading-time estimates increase reading completion.
